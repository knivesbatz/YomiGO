# YomiGo's Study Guide. Written in YomiGo's own words, drawing on the guide sites listed in docs/BIBLIOGRAPHY.md.
@ Your setup checklist
Do these once, a little at a time. You don't need them all on day one.
1. Add the Japanese keyboard to your PC or phone.
2. Install YomiGo Reader in your browser, to look up words on any page.
3. Pick a regular study time, and a daily amount you can keep up on a bad day.
4. Find one easy show, game, or channel you enjoy.
5. After hiragana, start adding words you meet in real Japanese to your own cards: Dict, Text Reader, and YomiGo Reader all have Add.
6. Once you know some grammar, practise in Talk, and then with real people (see Speaking with real people).
@ What a good day looks like
Reviews first, every day, even if you skip the class.
Then one class at your chosen pace.
Then Japanese you enjoy: as little as 15 minutes at first, growing toward 1 to 2 hours a day as you can.
A common target once you're past the basics is about 10 new words and 2 grammar points a day. Expect around ten reviews for each new card.
@ If you fall behind
Lower your pace in Settings. Keep doing your reviews.
Nearly every guide agrees that new cards are the thing to cut. Skipped reviews are what make people give up.
Tired today? Watch or play something easy instead of doing nothing. It still counts.
@ Listening
Start listening early, before you understand it. You're learning the rhythm and sounds.
Active listening means you watch and pay attention. It teaches the most.
Passive listening means audio plays while you do chores. It fills gaps in your day.
When listening, go with the flow. Look up only words that stand out or keep coming back.
Beginners often do more listening than reading, roughly 7 parts to 3, moving toward an even split.
Once you can read kana, Japanese subtitles help: you hear and see each line at once. On the PC, Transcribe makes Japanese subtitles for your own videos.
@ Reading
Start as soon as you can read kana, even if you understand little. Graded readers and kana-only games work well.
Reading is slower: read, look it up, read it again.
Most guides put reading at the centre of long-term progress. Vary what you read, or stick to everyday-life stories, which use the most useful words.
@ Sentence mining
Once you've finished the beginner stage, make your own flashcards from real Japanese. Save each word with the sentence you met it in.
YomiGo Reader does this in your browser, and exports to Anki.
Words you've met in something you care about stick far better than words from a list.
@ Saying I, you, he and she
わたし (私) works for everyone, in any situation. わたくし is more formal still, for work and speeches.
Casually, many men and boys say ぼく (僕), and おれ (俺) with friends, which sounds rough. Many women and girls say あたし. Some women and some nonbinary people say ぼく too.
Japanese leaves out "I" once it's clear, so you'll say it far less than in English.
"You" is mostly left out too. あなた can sound distant or cold, so use the person's name with さん: 田中さんは学生ですか。
きみ and おまえ are casual, and can sound rude to someone you don't know well.
かれ (彼) means he, and かのじょ (彼女) she, but they also mean boyfriend and girlfriend. Names are safer.
@ Grammar and kanji
Read grammar to understand it, not to memorise it. It won't stick the first time, and that's normal. If an explanation confuses you, try another guide.
Learn kanji through words. Guides warn against studying kanji only on their own for long. Mnemonics help when kanji look alike, which is why every YomiGo kanji card has a memory hook.
@ Mistakes to avoid
Rushing: too many new cards a day leads to burnout.
Skipping reviews.
Staying on beginner material too long, or waiting until you feel ready for real Japanese.
Polishing small details instead of moving on.
Relying on any one app alone, including this one. Real Japanese and real people are the goal.
@ Where the guides disagree
Handwriting: some say to practise kana by hand, and many say handwriting kanji is optional unless you want it. YomiGo has you write kana, and leaves kanji up to you.
Speaking: some leave it for later, and others warn against waiting. YomiGo has you speak from the start, because confidence grows with use.
Kanji courses: some like dedicated courses, others prefer words only. YomiGo uses small sets tied to words.
Language apps: several guides warn against relying on them. That's why YomiGo keeps sending you to real Japanese.
@ Pitch accent
Japanese words have a pattern of high and low pitch, not a stressed syllable. Saying it with the wrong pattern rarely stops you being understood, but the right one makes you easier to follow and sound natural.
In standard Tokyo Japanese, the first two sounds of a word differ: low then high, unless the word starts high and drops at once.
A word's pitch drops after one particular sound, or never. Where it drops sorts words into four kinds: 頭高 (drops after the first sound), 中高 (drops inside the word), 尾高 (drops at the very end, heard on the particle after it), and 平板 (never drops).
The classic example: はし said high-low is 箸, chopsticks. Low-high with the particle dropping, はしが, is 橋, a bridge. Low-high with the particle staying high is 端, an edge. Likewise 雨 (rain) starts high, and 飴 (a sweet) starts low.
To learn it, listen closely and copy: shadowing, the Speaking button in classes, trains it. Notice the pitch of new words as you meet them, and record yourself to compare.
Don't let it slow you down early. Many learners train their ear first, and tidy up their own speech later.
@ Reading lots of easy Japanese
You learn to read by reading a lot of easy Japanese, not a little hard Japanese. Graded readers exist for this: short stories written in levels, the first using only hiragana.
Many reading programmes share a few rules: start below your level, don't look up every word, skip what you don't follow, and drop anything boring for something else.
Then move on to easy news written for learners and children, with furigana; then to manga and games you know well; then to novels.
YomiGo's Reading steps are graded stories. The Text Reader turns anything you copy or screenshot into words you can look up.
Read a little every day. Speed and confidence come from how much you read.
@ Using YomiGo with real Japanese
Web pages: YomiGo Reader shows a word's meaning when you point at it, and saves it with its sentence.
Games and visual novels: a text hooker copies the game's text to the clipboard, and the Text Reader picks it up so you can look up every word.
Manga and screenshots: the Text Reader reads the text in a screenshot or photo. It does best with horizontal text for now.
Your own videos: Transcribe, on the PC, makes Japanese subtitles from your audio and video files.
Your own flashcards: Add puts a word into your reviews from Dict, the Text Reader, or Reader. Reader can also export to Anki, if you use it.
The dictionary: Dict has about 22,000 common words from JMdict, the open dictionary most Japanese tools are built on.
@ Speaking with real people
Sakura in Talk is good practice, but she's a program. Real people are the goal.
There are two common ways to find them. In a language exchange, you help someone with your language and they help you with Japanese, often free, through apps or local meetups. A paid tutor plans lessons around you.
Start with a set topic: introduce yourself, talk about your day, or describe a photo. Write a few sentences beforehand.
Ask your partner to correct you and to slow down. Write down new words, and add them to your cards afterwards.
Don't share personal details online that you wouldn't give a stranger.
@ The JLPT levels
The Japanese-Language Proficiency Test has five levels. N5 is the easiest and N1 the hardest.
N5: simple phrases and sentences in hiragana, katakana, and basic kanji. N4: everyday conversation spoken slowly, and reading about familiar things. N3: everyday Japanese at close to natural speed, and short articles. N2: news, articles, and most conversation at natural speed. N1: complex writing and fast speech on almost any subject.
Commonly quoted estimates, not official numbers: N5 about 800 words and 100 kanji, N4 about 1,500 and 300, N3 about 3,750 and 650, N2 about 6,000 and 1,000, N1 about 10,000 and 2,000. The test has no official word list.
It tests reading and listening only, by multiple choice. There's no speaking or writing.
In Japan it's held twice a year, in July and December. Elsewhere, ask your local test centre: many hold it once a year.
To pass, you need a total score and a minimum in each section.
YomiGo's exams aren't the JLPT. They check what each YomiGo stage taught.
